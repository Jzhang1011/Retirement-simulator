The Old Playbook is Broken

The yen has been fascinating lately, and the market is finally reaching an important turning point[cite: 3]. Recently, Japan spent ¥15.4 trillion, or about $96.5 billion, supporting the yen in the foreign-exchange market[cite: 4]. Yet the currency has slipped back toward ¥160 per dollar[cite: 5]. The intervention worked for a moment, but it did not fix the underlying problem[cite: 6]. Japan cannot permanently defend the yen with foreign-exchange reserves if the economics keep pushing money in the opposite direction[cite: 7].

The Shift in Capital

For years, the easy Japan trade was simple: buy exporters, enjoy a weak yen, and wait for corporate earnings to improve[cite: 12]. That playbook worked because the currency itself was doing part of the work[cite: 80]. A weak yen makes every dollar of overseas revenue worth more yen[cite: 32]. That creates an easy earnings boost without necessarily improving the underlying business[cite: 33].

But the environment is changing[cite: 81]. Japan spent decades with extremely low rates while the U.S. offered much higher yields[cite: 18]. Now the Bank of Japan is facing pressure to raise rates again[cite: 8]. Economists increasingly expect the policy rate to reach 1.25% in September, after reaching 1% in June[cite: 9]. At the same time, Japan's 10-year government bond yield has reached 3%, its highest level in roughly three decades[cite: 10].

When U.S. rates are high and Japanese rates are near zero, investors borrow cheap yen to buy higher-yielding assets elsewhere, leaving little incentive to hold the yen[cite: 19]. This dynamic is exactly what is shifting as Japan normalizes rates.

When the Tide Goes Out on Exporters

We can see the stress on traditional exporters already. Toyota's April-June revenue increased 10.4% to ¥13.5 trillion, but operating income declined from ¥1.166 trillion to ¥1.063 trillion[cite: 36]. The company is already dealing with tariffs, global competition, and currency sensitivity[cite: 37].

Honda shows the other side of the equation. Its April-June operating profit jumped to ¥530.8 billion, and it raised its full-year operating-profit forecast by 30% to ¥650 billion[cite: 42]. But part of that improvement came from the weaker yen, and Honda still expects another ¥520 billion of restructuring costs this fiscal year after its massive EV restructuring[cite: 43].

The Counterintuitive Truth

A weak yen can make a weak business look better[cite: 45]. A strong yen forces management to become better[cite: 46].

The Value Migration to Banks

If the real bottleneck is no longer access to cheap money, but whether companies can generate attractive returns on that money, megabanks may actually be one of the cleanest ways to express the structural change[cite: 90, 91, 94].

  • MUFG: First-quarter net profit jumped 48% year over year to ¥809.4 billion[cite: 95]. Its domestic loan spread increased to 1.15% from 0.95%, showing exactly what happens when Japan moves away from zero-rate economics[cite: 96].
  • Mizuho: Net profit rose 45% to ¥422.9 billion, while its domestic loan-deposit margin increased to 1.26% from 1.10%[cite: 97]. Mizuho also doubled its share buyback program to ¥200 billion[cite: 98].

A stronger yen hurts an exporter when foreign revenue gets translated back into fewer yen[cite: 100]. A higher rate can help a bank because the bank can earn more on its domestic assets[cite: 101]. This transition is healthy. When money costs something, management has to answer harder questions about capital allocation and corporate reform[cite: 165, 172, 176].
